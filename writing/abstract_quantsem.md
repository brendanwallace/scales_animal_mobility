A recent landmark human-mobility study reveals how a hidden structure of spatial 'containers' at different scales restricts human movements. The model infers, from a GPS trajectory, how a person conceptualizes neighborhoods, cities, regions, etc., and their probabilities of moving between them. This model resolves a contradiction between an empirical 'scale-free' distribution of trip lengths, and the scales – geographic, administrative and transportation modality – that describe and constrain human behavior.

This raises the complementary question: does animal movement more broadly contain spatial containers at different scales? Using animal trajectories from a range of taxa assembled from _Movebank_ and other sources, we show that indeed avian and terrestrial animals have spatial containers, and an identical characteristic number of scales. We fail to find similar structure for aquatic animals, which we argue may be due to differences in data collection or a more shifting aquatic landscape. We demonstrate that in walking animals, median speed and body length strongly predict the sizes of their containers; whereas we do not find a similar relationship in flying animals.
